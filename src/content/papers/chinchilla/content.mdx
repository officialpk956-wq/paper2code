# Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (Chinchilla)

**Hoffmann et al. · NeurIPS 2022 · arXiv:2203.15556 · DeepMind**

---

## 1. Overview

Chinchilla is the paper that proved the entire LLM industry was building models wrong. At the time of publication in March 2022, the field was racing to build ever-larger parameter counts — GPT-3 (175B), Gopher (280B), MT-NLG (530B). All were trained on datasets of 300B tokens or fewer.

Hoffmann et al. asked a different question: given a fixed compute budget, what is the **optimal** balance between model size and number of training tokens? Using 400 training runs spanning models from 70M to 16B parameters and 5B to 500B tokens, they derived a clean empirical law: **for compute-optimal training, the number of parameters and number of training tokens should scale equally.** Since GPT-3 was trained with far too few tokens for its parameter count, a much smaller model (Chinchilla, 70B) trained on 4× more data (1.4T tokens) matched or beat Gopher (280B) on every benchmark.

This paper redirected the entire field. LLaMA, Mistral, and every subsequent efficient model was built on Chinchilla's insight: data, not parameters, is the bottleneck.

---

## 2. Historical Context

OpenAI's scaling laws paper (Kaplan et al., 2020) had established that loss scales as a power law with compute, parameters, and tokens — and crucially concluded that **parameters should scale faster than data**. The field followed this prescription, building ever-larger models on relatively fixed datasets.

DeepMind had just trained Gopher (280B parameters) on 300B tokens — an enormous parameter-to-token ratio. Then they ran the Chinchilla experiments and discovered their own model was severely undertrained. The math showed that a 70B model on 1.4T tokens would match Gopher at a fraction of the inference cost.

---

## 3. Research Problem

Given a fixed compute budget $C$ (measured in FLOPs), what is the optimal number of model parameters $N^*$ and number of training tokens $D^*$?

Formally: minimize $L(N, D)$ subject to $C = 6ND$ (the approximation that one token forward-backward pass costs $6ND$ FLOPs).

---

## 4. Prior Work

| Scaling Law | Prediction | Reality |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Kaplan et al. (2020) | Scale parameters faster than data | Led to undertrained large models |
| Neural Scaling Laws (2022) | Power laws hold | Confirmed, but exponents revised |

**Kaplan's key error:** Their scaling law experiments held dataset size fixed when varying model size. This conflated two variables and produced biased recommendations.

---

## 5. Why Previous Methods Failed

Kaplan et al. (2020) measured loss as a function of parameters *while holding data size constant*. This is equivalent to testing how full your plate is while keeping the amount of food constant — you're measuring the wrong thing. When you correctly optimize the compute frontier (both $N$ and $D$ free to vary given fixed $C$), the optimal trade-off is very different.

Specifically, Kaplan's law suggested $N \propto C^{0.73}$ and $D \propto C^{0.27}$. Chinchilla found $N \propto C^{0.5}$ and $D \propto C^{0.5}$ — equal scaling.

---

## 6. Core Innovation

**Three methods, same conclusion:**

**Method 1:** Fix compute budget, vary $N$ and $D$ with $C = 6ND$ constant. Measure minimum loss.

**Method 2:** For each model size $N$, train on multiple token counts $D$ and fit the loss surface.

**Method 3:** Parametrize loss as:
$$\hat{L}(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$$

Fit $A, B, E, \alpha, \beta$ to all training runs. The compute-optimal frontier satisfies:
$$N_{opt} = G \cdot C^{0.5}, \quad D_{opt} = G^{-1} \cdot C^{0.5}$$

**Compute-optimal rule of thumb:**
$$\text{Tokens} = 20 \times \text{Parameters}$$

GPT-3 (175B) should have been trained on $20 \times 175\text{B} = 3.5\text{T}$ tokens. It was trained on 300B.

| Property | Value |
|:---|:---|
| Parameters | 70B |
| Training tokens | 1.4 Trillion |
| Architecture | Decoder-only Transformer |
| Context length | 2048 |

The architecture is unremarkable — the contribution is the *training compute allocation*, not architectural innovation.

---

## 8. Mathematical Foundations

**Parametric loss model:**
$$\hat{L}(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$$

Fitted values:
- $A = 406.4$, $\alpha = 0.34$
- $B = 410.7$, $\beta = 0.28$
- $E = 1.69$ (irreducible entropy of natural language)

**Compute-optimal $N$ and $D$:**

Taking $\partial \hat{L}/\partial N = 0$ and $\partial \hat{L}/\partial D = 0$ subject to $C = 6ND$:

$$N_{opt}(C) \propto C^{a/(a+b)}, \quad D_{opt}(C) \propto C^{b/(a+b)}$$

With $a = \alpha = 0.34$ and $b = \beta = 0.28$: $N_{opt} \propto C^{0.55}$, $D_{opt} \propto C^{0.45}$ — approximately equal.

---

## 9. Key Experiments

400 training runs were conducted, covering:
- Model sizes: 70M to 16B parameters
- Token counts: 5B to 500B
- IsoFLOP profiles (fixed compute, varied N and D)

**The critical result:** Plotting minimum loss along the compute-optimal frontier shows equal scaling of $N$ and $D$.

## 12. Implementation Details

- **Infrastructure:** Google TPU v3/v4 pods, JAX
- **Optimizer:** AdamW, cosine LR schedule
- **Data:** Same MassiveText corpus as Gopher (books, news, Wikipedia, GitHub, C4)
- **All 400 runs** were used to fit the loss surface (meta-learning at scale)

---

## 13. Engineering Insights

- **Inference cost ∝ parameters, not training tokens.** A 70B model is 4× cheaper to run than a 280B model. When you serve a model to millions of users, inference cost dominates lifetime compute cost. Chinchilla's insight means the efficient choice for deployment is always to train a smaller model for longer.
- **The 20× rule of thumb is actionable.** If you have 10B tokens of domain-specific text, the optimal model is 500M parameters. Not 7B.
- **Data quality matters more after Chinchilla.** Once you know data is the binding constraint, data quality engineering (filtering, deduplication, domain balancing) became the primary research focus.

---

## 14. Limitations

- **The compute-optimal frontier assumes training once.** If you're building a model to be used by millions of users, the optimal point shifts toward smaller models (lower inference cost) with even more training tokens — "beyond Chinchilla optimal."
- **Does not account for emergent capabilities.** Some capabilities emerge discontinuously with scale, meaning a slightly larger model trained slightly less may unlock capabilities that a smaller Chinchilla-optimal model lacks.
- **Dataset quality was held fixed.** Chinchilla's law was derived on MassiveText. Higher-quality data (like code, textbooks) shifts the optimum.
