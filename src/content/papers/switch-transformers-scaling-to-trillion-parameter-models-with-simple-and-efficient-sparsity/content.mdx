# Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity

**Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer · JMLR 2022 · arXiv:2101.03961**

---

## 1. Overview

Switch Transformers proved that trillion-parameter models could be trained with constant per-token compute cost by replacing the Feed-Forward Network (FFN) in each Transformer layer with a **Mixture of Experts (MoE)** that routes each token to exactly **one** expert — not two. This Top-1 routing (the "switch" in Switch Transformer) simplified prior MoE designs, eliminated routing complexity, and enabled 7× faster pre-training compared to a T5-equivalent dense baseline.

The paper broke the $10^{11}$ (100B) parameter barrier cleanly, training a 1.6 trillion parameter model on Google's TPU infrastructure. It established MoE as the path to efficiently scaling beyond the density constraints of hardware SRAM.

---

## 2. Historical Context

Mixture of Experts dates to 1991. Its application to deep learning was pioneered by Shazeer et al. (2017) with the Sparsely-Gated MoE LSTM (137B parameters). That paper showed the concept worked but left two unresolved problems: Top-K routing (usually K=2) added complexity, and training instability in sparse systems was hard to manage.

By 2020, the community had established that dense scaling was hitting physical limits. Training a 175B parameter dense model (GPT-3) required specialized hardware and weeks of compute. The question: could sparsity maintain the performance gains of scale without proportional compute costs?

---

## 3. Research Problem

Can we design a sparse expert model that:
1. Routes with $K=1$ (simpler than $K=2$, lower communication overhead)
2. Remains numerically stable during training
3. Balances load across experts without routing collapse
4. Scales to trillion parameters on existing hardware

## 5. Why Previous Methods Failed

**K=2 routing added overhead:** Every token dispatched to two experts, doubling communication costs in distributed settings where experts live on different devices.

**Routing collapse was common:** Without careful loss engineering, the router collapsed to always selecting one or two experts. Those experts got more gradients → got better → got more traffic → catastrophic collapse.

**Numerical instability:** MoE models with sparse activation had gradient and precision issues (NaN losses) during training, especially with FP16.

---

## 6. Core Innovation

**Top-1 Switch Routing:**

For each token $x$, compute routing probabilities:
$$p_i(x) = \text{softmax}(W_r \cdot x)_i$$

Route each token to the single expert with highest probability:
$$\text{Expert}_{\text{selected}} = \arg\max_i p_i(x)$$

Scale the expert output by the routing probability:
$$\text{output} = p_{\text{selected}} \cdot E_{\text{selected}}(x)$$

**Load Balancing Loss:**
To prevent collapse, add an auxiliary loss that encourages uniform routing:
$$L_{aux} = \alpha \cdot N \cdot \sum_{i=1}^N f_i \cdot P_i$$

Where $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens routed to expert $i$, and $P_i$ is the mean probability of routing to expert $i$. Minimized when $f_i = P_i = 1/N$ for all $i$.

**Expert Capacity:**
Each expert has a fixed buffer. If too many tokens route to one expert, the excess are passed through (skipped) via the residual connection. This prevents OOM errors.

**FP32 router, FP16 everything else:** Training exclusively in FP16 caused NaN losses from router softmax. The fix: compute the router in FP32, everything else in FP16.

---

## 7. Architecture Breakdown

Switch Transformer replaces every other FFN layer in T5 with a Switch FFN layer:

```
Standard T5 Layer:
  Input → Self-Attention → Add & Norm → FFN → Add & Norm → Output

Switch T5 Layer (alternate layers):
  Input → Self-Attention → Add & Norm → [Switch Router → Expert FFN] → Add & Norm → Output
```

**Model sizes:**

| Model | Parameters (sparse) | Active Params per Token | Experts per Layer |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Switch-Base | 7.4B | 223M | 128 |
| Switch-Large | 26.3B | 739M | 128 |
| Switch-C | **1.571T** | — | 2048 |

## 8. Mathematical Foundations

**Sparse Forward Pass (per token):**

Let $N$ be number of experts, $W_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$ be router weights, $E_i$ be expert $i$'s FFN.

$$h_i = \text{softmax}(W_r x)_i \quad \text{(routing probabilities)}$$
$$\text{output} = h_{\arg\max h} \cdot E_{\arg\max h}(x)$$

Only one expert FFN is evaluated — all others contribute 0 FLOPs.

**Capacity Factor $C$:**

Expert capacity = $\frac{\text{tokens per batch}}{N} \times C$

$C=1.0$ is exact balance. $C=1.25$ allows 25% overflow buffer. Tokens exceeding capacity are dropped and bypass via residual.

## 12. Implementation Details

- **Framework:** T5X (JAX/XLA)
- **Hardware:** TPU v3 or v4 pods
- **Expert placement:** Each expert on a separate TPU core; all-to-all communication for routing
- **Key stability fix:** FP32 precision for router logits; FP16 everywhere else
- **Dropout:** 0.1 in experts; 0.4 at fine-tuning time (higher dropout to regularize larger capacity)

---

## 13. Engineering Insights

- **All-to-all communication is the bottleneck.** Routing tokens to experts on remote TPU cores requires collective communication. In very large clusters (thousands of devices), this inter-device bandwidth becomes the limiting factor, not compute.
- **Expert capacity buffer prevents OOM but causes dropped tokens.** A capacity factor of 1.25 allows 25% more tokens than a perfectly balanced expert would receive. Tokens exceeding capacity are dropped. Monitoring the "fraction of dropped tokens" is critical.
- **Different experts learn different specializations.** Visualization shows experts develop loose specializations: some handle punctuation, others handle specific domains (science, news). Specialization emerges without explicit supervision.

---

## 14. Limitations

- **Memory bound, not compute bound.** MoE improves FLOPs-per-token but all expert weights must reside in SRAM. A 1.6T parameter model requires enormous memory bandwidth even if per-token FLOPs are low.
- **Communication overhead at scale.** All-to-all routing becomes a bottleneck in multi-host distributed training.
- **Fine-tuning instability.** MoE models fine-tuned on small datasets can suffer routing collapse without very careful loss engineering.
