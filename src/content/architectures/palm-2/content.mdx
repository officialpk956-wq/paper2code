# PaLM 2

## 1. Overview
PaLM 2 is Google's next-generation large language model, introduced in 2023 as a successor to the original PaLM. Unlike its predecessor, which was an exercise in extreme parameter scaling (540 Billion parameters), PaLM 2 represents a paradigm shift toward compute-optimal scaling and rigorous data engineering. It is a family of models delivered in various sizes (codenamed Gecko, Otter, Bison, and Unicorn) designed to be highly capable yet highly efficient to run, allowing deployment across a wide range of devices, including mobile phones. PaLM 2 significantly outperforms the original PaLM in advanced reasoning, coding, and mathematical tasks, despite the largest version likely being smaller in parameter count. This leap in performance was achieved through a compute-optimal training recipe heavily influenced by Chinchilla scaling laws, a vastly improved and heavily multilingual pre-training dataset, and architectural refinements. PaLM 2 powers many of Google's core AI features, including early versions of the Bard (now Gemini) chatbot and specialized models like Med-PaLM 2 and Sec-PaLM.

:::tip
PaLM 2 is an excellent example of the industry learning that "bigger isn't always better." Better data and longer training times on a smaller model often yield superior, more usable results than a massive, under-trained model.
:::

## 2. Historical Context
The development of PaLM 2 occurred during a period of rapid recalibration in the AI research community. In 2022, the original PaLM demonstrated that extreme scale (540B parameters) unlocked new capabilities. However, DeepMind's Chinchilla paper soon revealed that models like PaLM and GPT-3 were vastly under-trained; they had too many parameters for the amount of data they were trained on. The release of ChatGPT further intensified the competition, demanding models that were not just capable, but fast and cheap enough to serve to millions of users globally. PaLM 2 was Google's strategic response. It abandoned the pursuit of sheer parameter size in favor of building a highly optimized, compute-efficient foundation model. The focus shifted from infrastructure flexing (Pathways) to data curation, extending the training duration massively, and creating a diverse family of models rather than a single monolithic behemoth.

## 3. Problem It Solves
PaLM 2 primarily addresses the deployment and efficiency problems of massive LLMs while simultaneously improving reasoning capabilities. The original PaLM 540B was too large for widespread consumer API deployment or edge computing. By adopting compute-optimal scaling, PaLM 2 solves the inference cost problem; models like PaLM 2 Gecko are small enough to run entirely on-device, offering privacy and zero-latency text generation. Furthermore, previous models often struggled with non-English languages and complex logic. PaLM 2 solves this by significantly increasing the proportion of multilingual text, mathematics, and code in its pre-training mix. This allows it to understand idioms, translate nuanced text across dozens of languages seamlessly, and solve algorithmic programming challenges far better than its predecessor, making it a truly global and versatile reasoning engine.

## 5. Layer-by-Layer Breakdown
PaLM 2 retains much of the successful architectural DNA of PaLM 1 but introduces key optimizations. The input sequence begins at the **Token Embedding**, which is shared with the output projection layer to conserve parameters. 

The core consists of parallel **Transformer Blocks**. Like its predecessor, PaLM 2 computes the Attention and the Feed-Forward Network (FFN) in parallel to optimize TPU utilization. A critical update likely involves transitioning from Multi-Query Attention (MQA) to **Grouped-Query Attention (GQA)**. While MQA (1 KV head) is very fast, it can sometimes degrade model quality compared to Multi-Head Attention. GQA strikes a balance by grouping a small number of query heads to share a single KV head, maintaining much of MQA's speed while preserving higher reasoning quality. 

Positional information is handled by **Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE)**. The FFN continues to use the **SwiGLU** activation, which has become an industry standard for maximizing the representational capacity of the feed-forward layers. After the residual addition of the parallel paths, the sequence passes through all $N$ blocks, ending with a **Final Layer Norm** and the **Linear Head** to generate next-token probabilities.

## 6. Tensor Flow Walkthrough
Let's trace the tensor shapes through a PaLM 2 block, specifically focusing on the Grouped-Query Attention (GQA). Assume Batch `B=4`, Sequence `T=1024`, `D_model=4096`, Number of Query Heads `H_q=32`, Number of KV Heads `H_kv=8`.

1.  **Input to Block:** `X`
    *   Shape: `(B, T, D_model)` -> `(4, 1024, 4096)`
2.  **Layer Normalization:** `X_norm = LN(X)`
    *   Shape: `(B, T, D_model)` -> `(4, 1024, 4096)`
3.  **Grouped-Query Attention (GQA):**
    *   Head Dimension: `D_head = D_model / H_q = 128`
    *   `Q` projection: `(B, T, H_q, D_head)` -> `(4, 1024, 32, 128)`
    *   `K, V` projections (fewer heads): `(B, T, H_kv, D_head)` -> `(4, 1024, 8, 128)`
    *   *Mechanism:* Each KV head is shared by `H_q / H_kv = 4` query heads. The KV tensors are conceptually broadcasted during the attention calculation.
    *   After attention and output projection: `Attn_out`
    *   Shape: `(B, T, D_model)` -> `(4, 1024, 4096)`
4.  **Parallel FFN (SwiGLU):**
    *   `FFN_out = SwiGLU_FFN(X_norm)`
    *   Shape: `(B, T, D_model)` -> `(4, 1024, 4096)`
5.  **Residual Addition:**
    *   `X_out = X + Attn_out + FFN_out`
    *   Shape: `(B, T, D_model)` -> `(4, 1024, 4096)`

## 7. Mathematical Foundations
The architectural optimizations of PaLM 2 center heavily around Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) to balance quality and inference speed.

In standard Multi-Head Attention (MHA), we have $H$ queries, $H$ keys, and $H$ values.
In Multi-Query Attention (MQA, used in PaLM 1), we have $H$ queries, $1$ key, and $1$ value.

In **Grouped-Query Attention (GQA)**, we divide the $H$ query heads into $G$ groups. Each group shares a single Key and Value head. Let $H_q$ be the number of query heads, and $H_{kv} = G$ be the number of KV heads.

For a specific query head $i$ belonging to group $g$ (where $g = \lfloor i \times \frac{H_{kv}}{H_q} \rfloor$):

$$ Q_i = xW_q^{(i)} $$
$$ K_g = xW_k^{(g)} $$
$$ V_g = xW_v^{(g)} $$

The attention for head $i$ is then calculated as:

$$ \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(Q_i, K_g, V_g) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_i K_g^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_g $$

By setting $1 < H_{kv} < H_q$, GQA interpolates smoothly between MQA and MHA. It significantly reduces the size of the KV cache compared to MHA (saving memory bandwidth during generation), while capturing more complex representational nuances than MQA, directly contributing to PaLM 2's improved reasoning capabilities without sacrificing the speed needed for real-time APIs.

## 8. Training Procedure
PaLM 2's training procedure marks a departure from its predecessor by heavily focusing on the **Chinchilla scaling laws**. Instead of scaling parameters independently, Google scaled the model size and the training dataset size in tandem. While the exact parameter counts are undisclosed, the largest PaLM 2 model is believed to be significantly smaller than 540B but trained on trillions of tokens—vastly more data than PaLM 1.

**Data Mixture:** The pre-training dataset was meticulously curated. It featured a significantly higher proportion of parallel multilingual text, which trained the model to understand context and translate idioms flawlessly rather than doing literal word-for-word translation. Furthermore, the dataset was enriched with mathematical equations, scientific papers, and extensive source code from various programming languages. This specific data mixture is responsible for PaLM 2's emergent capabilities in logic and reasoning.

**Objectives:** While primarily trained with standard causal language modeling (predicting the next token), the PaLM 2 technical report mentions utilizing a diverse set of pre-training objectives. This likely includes mixture-of-denoisers (similar to UL2 training), where the model sometimes predicts masked spans of text. This hybrid objective approach improves the model's ability to handle both generative tasks (writing) and representation tasks (understanding context and filling in blanks).

## 9. PyTorch Implementation
Below is a conceptual PyTorch implementation focusing on Grouped-Query Attention (GQA), a key feature distinguishing modern efficient architectures like PaLM 2.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class GroupedQueryAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, num_query_heads, num_kv_heads):
        super().__init__()
        assert d_model % num_query_heads == 0, "d_model must be divisible by query heads"
        assert num_query_heads % num_kv_heads == 0, "query heads must be divisible by kv heads"
        
        self.d_model = d_model
        self.n_q = num_query_heads
        self.n_kv = num_kv_heads
        self.d_head = d_model // num_query_heads
        self.num_groups = num_query_heads // num_kv_heads
        
        # Projections
        self.w_q = nn.Linear(d_model, num_query_heads * self.d_head, bias=False)
        self.w_k = nn.Linear(d_model, num_kv_heads * self.d_head, bias=False)
        self.w_v = nn.Linear(d_model, num_kv_heads * self.d_head, bias=False)
        self.w_o = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model, bias=False)

    def forward(self, x):
        B, T, C = x.size()
        
        # Project and reshape: (B, T, Heads, D_head) -> (B, Heads, T, D_head)
        q = self.w_q(x).view(B, T, self.n_q, self.d_head).transpose(1, 2)
        k = self.w_k(x).view(B, T, self.n_kv, self.d_head).transpose(1, 2)
        v = self.w_v(x).view(B, T, self.n_kv, self.d_head).transpose(1, 2)
        
        # Expand K and V to match the number of Query heads
        # Shape becomes: (B, n_q, T, d_head)
        k_expanded = k.repeat_interleave(self.num_groups, dim=1)
        v_expanded = v.repeat_interleave(self.num_groups, dim=1)
        
        # Attention calculation
        # q: (B, n_q, T, d_head) @ k_exp^T: (B, n_q, d_head, T) -> (B, n_q, T, T)
        scores = torch.matmul(q, k_expanded.transpose(-2, -1)) / (self.d_head ** 0.5)
        attn = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)
        
        # Output calculation
        # attn: (B, n_q, T, T) @ v_exp: (B, n_q, T, d_head) -> (B, n_q, T, d_head)
        out = torch.matmul(attn, v_expanded)
        
        # Reshape back to (B, T, d_model)
        out = out.transpose(1, 2).contiguous().view(B, T, self.d_model)
        return self.w_o(out)

# --- Test ---
if __name__ == "__main__":
    B, T, d_model = 2, 10, 128
    num_q_heads, num_kv_heads = 8, 2
    
    gqa = GroupedQueryAttention(d_model, num_q_heads, num_kv_heads)
    x = torch.randn(B, T, d_model)
    out = gqa(x)
    
    print(f"Input shape: {x.shape}")
    print(f"Output shape: {out.shape}")
    assert out.shape == (B, T, d_model), "Shape mismatch!"
    print(f"Test passed: GQA handled {num_q_heads} Q heads with {num_kv_heads} KV heads.")
```

## 10. Strengths
PaLM 2's greatest strength is its efficiency and deployability. By adhering to compute-optimal scaling, Google created a highly capable model that is significantly cheaper and faster to run than PaLM 1, allowing it to power numerous consumer-facing products with low latency. Its multilingual capabilities are exceptional; because it was trained on vast amounts of parallel text, it performs nuanced, culturally aware translations that rival dedicated translation models. Furthermore, its proficiency in logic, math, and coding is dramatically improved due to targeted data curation. The availability of PaLM 2 in various sizes (Gecko to Unicorn) provides immense flexibility, allowing developers to choose the exact trade-off between capability and compute cost for their specific use case.

## 11. Weaknesses
While highly efficient, PaLM 2 is essentially an optimization of the standard LLM paradigm rather than a radical architectural departure. Like all LLMs, it remains susceptible to hallucinations and confident factual errors. As a primarily text-based model (in its base form), it lacked the native omni-modality seen in later models like GPT-4o, processing images via separate pipeline integrations rather than natively in the same latent space. Finally, Google has been highly secretive about the exact parameter counts and dataset sizes of the PaLM 2 family, making it difficult for the open-source community to replicate or thoroughly analyze the specific scaling laws implemented.

## 12. Research Evolution
PaLM 2 represents the maturation of the LLM scaling era. It embodies the transition from "scale parameters at all costs" (PaLM 1) to "scale compute optimally" (Chinchilla). It reflects an industry-wide realization that data quality—specifically the inclusion of math, code, and diverse languages—is just as critical as architectural tweaks for unlocking reasoning capabilities. PaLM 2 also highlights the shift towards building model families rather than single behemoths, acknowledging that edge devices require fundamentally different architectures than cloud APIs. This compute-optimal, data-heavy approach directly paved the way for Google's subsequent Gemini family of models, which adopted PaLM 2's training philosophies while finally integrating native multimodality from the ground up.

## 13. Interview Questions

**Q1: How did the Chinchilla scaling laws influence the development of PaLM 2 compared to PaLM 1?**
**A1:** PaLM 1 (540B parameters, 780B tokens) was built under the assumption that increasing parameter count was the primary driver of performance. Chinchilla proved that model size and training data must scale equally. An over-parameterized model trained on too little data is computationally wasteful. PaLM 2 applied this logic; it is a significantly smaller model than PaLM 1 but was trained on trillions of tokens (a much longer duration relative to its size). This compute-optimal approach yielded a model that is both smarter and much cheaper to run.

**Q2: Describe Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) and why PaLM 2 might prefer it over Multi-Query Attention (MQA).**
**A2:** MQA uses one Key/Value head for all Query heads. This makes the KV cache tiny, resulting in blazing fast inference, but it can bottleneck the model's representational capacity and degrade quality slightly. GQA is a compromise: it groups Query heads (e.g., 8 Q heads share 1 KV head). This significantly reduces the KV cache size compared to standard attention (improving speed and memory bandwidth) while maintaining multiple KV representations, preserving the higher reasoning quality that might be lost with pure MQA.

**Q3: Why did PaLM 2 show such a dramatic improvement in coding and mathematics compared to its predecessor?**
**A3:** The improvement wasn't primarily due to architecture, but data. Google intentionally curated the PaLM 2 pre-training dataset to include a vastly higher proportion of source code across numerous languages and scientific papers containing mathematical equations. Models learn what they are fed; by exposing the model to the strict syntactic rules of programming languages and the logical structure of math during pre-training, the model developed stronger generalized reasoning and deduction capabilities.

**Q4: PaLM 2 utilizes a "Mixture-of-Denoisers" objective. What does this mean and how does it differ from standard next-token prediction?**
**A4:** Standard causal language modeling only predicts the next word given the previous words (autoregressive). Mixture-of-Denoisers (like UL2) trains the model on multiple tasks simultaneously. While it does standard next-token prediction, it also does masked language modeling (predicting a masked word in the middle of a sentence) and prefix language modeling. This forces the model to learn bidirectional context (understanding what comes before *and* after a word), which improves its performance on diverse tasks beyond just text generation.

**Q5: What is the significance of the PaLM 2 "Gecko" model?**
**A5:** Gecko is the smallest model in the PaLM 2 family. Its significance lies in edge computing. Because it was trained using compute-optimal scaling laws, it punches far above its weight class. Gecko is small enough to run entirely on a modern mobile device without an internet connection. This enables fast, completely private AI processing for tasks like on-device translation, summarization, and autocomplete, representing a massive shift away from relying solely on expensive cloud infrastructure.
