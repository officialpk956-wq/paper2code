# Generative Models Taxonomy

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
Generative AI is not just Transformers and Diffusion. Historically, researchers have attacked the problem of generating new data through multiple distinct mathematical frameworks, each trading off between generation quality, sampling speed, and exact likelihood calculation.
</Callout>

## Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

A standard Autoencoder compresses an image into a tiny vector and then decompresses it. But you cannot generate *new* images because the latent space is a disjointed mess.

**Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)** fix this by forcing the latent space to follow a continuous probability distribution (usually a standard Gaussian/Normal distribution). Instead of outputting a single vector, the Encoder outputs two vectors: a mean ($\mu$) and a standard deviation ($\sigma$). The model then samples a point from this distribution to feed to the Decoder.

<SVGDiagram src="/diagrams/generative-models-taxonomy.svg" caption="The VAE architecture with the Reparameterization Trick." />

**The Reparameterization Trick:** You cannot backpropagate gradients through a random sampling process. To solve this, VAEs reparameterize the sample as $Z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is a random noise constant. Now, the gradients can flow smoothly through $\mu$ and $\sigma$ to train the network.

**VQ-VAE (Vector Quantized VAE):** An evolution of the VAE that uses a discrete codebook instead of a continuous distribution. It prevents posterior collapse and generates incredibly crisp images, forming the foundation of models like Midjourney and Stable Diffusion (which use VAEs to compress images before running diffusion).

## Normalizing Flows

While VAEs optimize an *approximate* lower bound (ELBO), **Normalizing Flows** (like GLOW and RealNVP) compute the *exact* probability likelihood of the data.

They achieve this through a mathematical trick called the **Change of Variables** theorem. A Normalizing Flow starts with a simple distribution (like Gaussian noise) and applies a sequence of complex, invertible neural network layers to morph it into the data distribution (like human faces). 

Because every layer must be strictly invertible and the determinant of the Jacobian must be easy to compute, Flow models are mathematically beautiful but architecturally restrictive and very memory-heavy.

## Energy-Based Models (EBMs)

Unlike VAEs or Flows, **Energy-Based Models** do not try to generate data directly. Instead, they learn a scalar "energy" function, $E(x)$. 

The network is trained so that real, high-quality images are assigned a low energy score (like a ball resting in a valley), and fake or corrupted images are assigned a high energy score (like a ball on a hill). To generate an image, you start with random noise and use gradient descent (Langevin dynamics) to slowly push the pixels downhill until they reach a low-energy state (a realistic image). EBMs generate stunningly high-quality samples but are painfully slow to run.

## Score Matching

Score Matching is the direct mathematical predecessor to modern Diffusion models. Instead of calculating the energy function, the model learns the **score function**, which is the gradient (the slope) of the log probability density. 

By knowing which direction is "downhill" toward real data at every point in the space, the model can start from pure noise and iteratively follow the score gradients to denoise the image.
