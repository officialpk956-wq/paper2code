# CNN Evolution: AlexNet to ConvNeXt

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
Every major CNN architecture solved one specific problem that was limiting the previous generation. AlexNet solved depth with GPU training. VGG solved filter size. ResNet solved vanishing gradients. EfficientNet solved compound scaling. ConvNeXt solved the question of whether CNNs could compete with Vision Transformers. Understanding CNNs means understanding the progression of problems and their solutions.
</Callout>

## 1998–2012: The Pre-Deep Learning Era

**LeNet-5 (LeCun, 1998)** is where it all began. Yann LeCun's network for handwritten digit recognition introduced the core ideas: convolutional layers for local pattern detection, pooling layers for spatial invariance, fully connected layers for classification. Running on a CPU, LeNet achieved near-human accuracy on handwritten digits.

The problem: LeNet couldn't scale. Deeper networks suffered from vanishing gradients. Training on natural image datasets (much harder than digits) required compute that CPUs couldn't provide.

## 2012: The AlexNet Shock

**AlexNet (Krizhevsky, 2012)** didn't just win ImageNet — it won by 10.9 percentage points over the next best entry, which had been built by teams spending years engineering hand-crafted features. The deep learning era began at that moment.

What made AlexNet work where previous deep networks failed:

**GPU training** — Training on two GTX 580 GPUs in parallel reduced the time from years to weeks. GPU parallelism made deeper networks tractable.

**ReLU activation** — Previous networks used tanh or sigmoid. ReLU ($f(x) = \max(0, x)$) doesn't saturate for positive values, which keeps gradients flowing in deep networks. Training was 6× faster than equivalent tanh networks.

**Dropout** — Randomly zeroing 50% of neuron outputs during training prevents co-adaptation (neurons learning to correct each other's mistakes). This is approximate model ensembling at training time.

**Data augmentation** — Random crops, horizontal flips, and color jitter at training time. The model never sees the same image twice, dramatically reducing overfitting.

## 2014: VGGNet — Depth Over Filter Size

**VGGNet (Simonyan, Zisserman, 2014)** asked a simple question: what if we just stack more 3×3 convolutions instead of using larger filters?

The insight: two 3×3 convolutions have the same receptive field as one 5×5 convolution, but use fewer parameters and introduce an extra non-linearity. Three 3×3 convolutions match a 7×7 receptive field but again with fewer parameters.

VGG's contribution wasn't a novel architectural idea — it was empirical evidence that depth (more layers) mattered more than filter size. VGG-16 and VGG-19 became the standard feature extractors for transfer learning for years. Their simplicity (just 3×3 convolutions and 2×2 max-pooling, repeated) made them easy to understand and modify.

The problem VGG left unsolved: training 16–19 layers was difficult. Adding more layers beyond VGG's depth made models worse — not from overfitting, but from training difficulty.

## 2015: ResNet — The Skip Connection Revolution

**ResNet (He, Zhang, Ren, Sun, 2015)** solved the degradation problem with one elegant idea: residual connections.

Instead of learning $H(x)$ (the desired transformation), a residual block learns $F(x) = H(x) - x$ (the residual). The skip connection adds the input directly to the output:

$$\text{output} = F(x, \{W_i\}) + x$$

Why this works: if a layer doesn't need to do anything, $F(x) \approx 0$ and the block becomes an identity mapping. This makes it easy to train the network to leave things unchanged when that's optimal, and only learn transformations that are genuinely beneficial. Gradients flow directly through skip connections, bypassing deep layer stacks.

The result: ResNet-152 (152 layers) trained stably and outperformed VGG-16 despite being 9× deeper. Skip connections became the most important architectural primitive in deep learning — they appear in every subsequent architecture including Transformers.

## 2017–2018: The Efficiency Wave

As deployment on mobile and edge devices became important, the field shifted from "how do we train bigger models?" to "how do we make models faster?"

**MobileNet (Howard et al., 2017)** introduced **depthwise separable convolutions** — factorizing a standard 3×3 convolution into a 3×3 depthwise convolution (one filter per input channel) followed by a 1×1 pointwise convolution (mixing channels). This reduces computation by 8–9× at the cost of a few percent accuracy.

**SENet (Hu et al., 2017)** introduced **Squeeze-and-Excitation** blocks — channel attention. A global average pool "squeezes" each feature map to a single number (the global average), a small fully-connected network learns which channels to emphasize, and the channel-wise weights are applied back to the feature maps. SENet won ILSVRC 2017 by adding this module to existing architectures with minimal compute overhead.

<Callout type="info" title="The 3×3 Convolution Dominance">
Nearly every modern CNN uses exclusively 3×3 convolutions. A 3×3 filter captures a 3×3 spatial neighborhood, connects to 9 input values per output, and can be stacked to produce any receptive field. The uniformity means highly optimized GPU kernels exist for 3×3 convolutions specifically — it's not just theoretically elegant, it's the fastest option on real hardware.
</Callout>

## 2019: EfficientNet — Compound Scaling

**EfficientNet (Tan, Le, 2019)** asked: if you have a fixed compute budget, what's the optimal way to allocate it between width (more channels), depth (more layers), and resolution (larger input)?

The answer, derived empirically with Neural Architecture Search: scale all three dimensions together with fixed ratios. If you double the compute budget, increase depth by 1.15×, width by 1.1×, and resolution by 1.15×. This **compound scaling** produces models along a Pareto frontier of accuracy vs. FLOPs.

EfficientNet-B7 achieved 84.4% ImageNet accuracy with 600M FLOPs — better than ResNet-152 at 11B FLOPs. The efficiency improvement was roughly 8.4× — the same accuracy at 8× less compute.

## 2022: ConvNeXt — CNNs Fight Back

When Vision Transformers (ViT, 2020) began outperforming CNNs on image benchmarks, it seemed like convolutions were obsolete. **ConvNeXt (Liu et al., 2022)** disproved this by modernizing ResNet with Transformer-inspired design choices.

The changes: depthwise 7×7 convolutions (instead of 3×3), inverted bottleneck blocks (wide in the middle, narrow at the edges — like Transformers), GELU activation (instead of ReLU), Layer Normalization (instead of Batch Normalization), and fewer but wider stages.

Each change was motivated by asking "what does the Vision Transformer do that ResNet doesn't?" — then implementing the equivalent CNN operation. The result: ConvNeXt matched Swin Transformer performance on ImageNet, COCO, and ADE20K with comparable compute, proving that the architectural ideas matter more than whether you use convolutions or attention.

<Callout type="warning" title="The Inductive Bias Tradeoff">
CNNs have hard-coded spatial inductive biases: locality (each neuron sees a local patch), translation equivariance (shifting the image shifts the features), and hierarchy (early layers detect edges, later layers detect objects). Vision Transformers have none of these — they learn everything from data. CNNs train better with less data. ViTs train better with more data. At the scale of JFT-300M (300M images), ViTs win. At the scale of ImageNet (1.2M images), CNNs are competitive or better.
</Callout>

## The Lineage Map

Understanding CNN evolution means tracing the key ancestry:

- LeNet → AlexNet → VGGNet → ResNet (main trunk)
- ResNet → ResNeXt → SENet → EfficientNet (efficiency branch)
- MobileNet → MobileNet v2 → MobileNet v3 (mobile branch)
- ResNet → DenseNet (extreme skip connections)
- ResNet → ConvNeXt (Transformer-modernized CNN)

Skip connections (ResNet) appear in almost every branch. The field converged on the idea that identity mappings should be possible at every depth — and everything after ResNet builds on that foundation.

## Practice

<Callout type="info">
The best way to understand CNN evolution is to implement each architecture from scratch. Start with a minimal ResNet block in PyTorch (3 lines of code for the skip connection), then add the SE block, then try the ConvNeXt inverted bottleneck. The architecture papers are all publicly available and their key sections are short — AlexNet's architecture description is one paragraph.
</Callout>
