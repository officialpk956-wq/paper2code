# Netflix Architecture: Microservices, Chaos, and Open Connect

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
Netflix is not a streaming company that uses software. Netflix is a software company that happens to stream video. Every frame you watch, every thumbnail you see, the exact order of rows on your homepage — all of it is controlled by software systems making real-time decisions. Understanding Netflix means understanding those systems.
</Callout>

## From One Database to 700 Microservices

In 2008, Netflix ran on a single Oracle database. It went down for three days. That outage triggered an eight-year migration away from the monolith, completed in 2016, that turned Netflix into the canonical example of microservices architecture.

Today Netflix runs on AWS with 700+ microservices, each owned by an independent team. The **title service** knows what content exists. The **user service** stores subscriber profiles and preferences. The **recommendation service** decides what to show you. The **streaming service** delivers the video. The **billing service** handles your subscription. Each service has its own database, its own deployment pipeline, and its own on-call rotation.

The advantage: teams deploy independently, often hundreds of times per day. A bug in the recommendation service doesn't take down streaming. The disadvantage: distributed systems fail in more complex ways than monoliths.

## Open Connect: Netflix's Private CDN

Netflix built their own CDN rather than paying Akamai or CloudFront. The result is **Open Connect** — a network of dedicated appliances (servers with large storage arrays) that Netflix places inside or adjacent to ISPs, for free, in exchange for the ISP serving Netflix traffic locally.

A user in Toronto watching a movie doesn't route to Netflix's Oregon data center. Their traffic goes to an Open Connect appliance at their ISP, potentially in the same building as their home gateway. The round-trip latency drops from 50–100ms to under 5ms. Segment fetch time goes from hundreds of milliseconds to tens of milliseconds. Buffering drops to near zero.

Netflix pre-loads the appliances nightly with the content expected to be popular the next day — based on their recommendation models predicting what subscribers will watch. Popular recent content is 100% cached. Long-tail library content falls back to regional AWS cache tiers, with true origin (S3) as the last resort.

The result: 95% of Netflix traffic is served from Open Connect without ever touching Netflix's origin infrastructure. This eliminates the majority of CDN egress costs and dramatically reduces backbone transit bandwidth.

<Callout type="info" title="Why Not Just Use AWS CloudFront?">
At Netflix's scale (petabytes per day), CloudFront pricing would be enormous. More importantly, Open Connect gives Netflix control — they can pre-position content based on their own prediction models, optimize appliance placement for their specific geographic traffic patterns, and tune caching algorithms for streaming video (which has very different access patterns than web content). Self-building CDN at scale is justified when you're delivering 2+ hours per subscriber per day.
</Callout>

## Chaos Engineering

In 2011, Netflix engineer Greg Hoffman wrote a small tool that randomly terminated EC2 instances in production. They named it Chaos Monkey.

The reasoning was counterintuitive: if you know your services will randomly fail (because cloud infrastructure does fail), you should build systems that handle failure gracefully. If you never test failure recovery, you'll discover your failure modes during a real incident. If you deliberately cause failures every day, you find and fix them before they cause outages.

Chaos Monkey evolved into a full Simian Army: Chaos Gorilla kills entire availability zones, Latency Monkey introduces artificial delay, Conformity Monkey finds services not following best practices, Security Monkey identifies misconfigured security groups. The entire practice became known as Chaos Engineering and is now standard at Amazon, Microsoft, and Google.

## Per-Title Encoding and VMAF

Different content types compress differently. An animation with solid color blocks (Studio Ghibli) is trivially compressible. A night-time action sequence with constant motion and dark gradients is much harder to compress. Encoding both at the same bitrate ladder wastes bandwidth on the animation and degrades quality on the action sequence.

Netflix's **per-title encoding** (2015) solves this by analyzing each title and computing its optimal bitrate ladder. The animation might look excellent at 1 Mbps; the action movie needs 4 Mbps for equivalent quality. Users on the animation use less bandwidth, improving streaming reliability. Users on the action movie get noticeably better quality at the same file size.

Quality is measured with VMAF (Video Multi-method Assessment Fusion), Netflix's own metric developed because standard metrics (PSNR, SSIM) didn't correlate well with human quality perception. VMAF combines multiple quality estimators trained on crowdsourced human ratings. It became so accurate that Netflix licenses it as an open-source tool now used industry-wide.

## The A/B Testing Machine

Netflix runs 100+ A/B experiments simultaneously. Every decision — thumbnail art, row ordering, recommendation algorithm, even the wording of cancellation flows — is tested against a control group before rolling out.

The experiments run at the **user level**: a user is assigned to a treatment group (by hashing their user ID) and sees a consistent experience within a session. Events are collected via Kafka streams, aggregated in near-real-time, and analyzed using Netflix's internal Causal Impact framework. Statistical significance is evaluated with Bayesian methods (faster than frequentist approaches for making ship decisions).

The most famous result: **thumbnail personalization**. Netflix found that showing a user a different thumbnail for the same title can increase click-through by 20–30%. A user who watches a lot of romcoms sees the romantic moment from a thriller. A user who watches a lot of action movies sees the fight scene. The same content, personalized presentation, dramatically different engagement. Netflix implemented this with a contextual multi-armed bandit.

## Real-World Case Studies

**The 2008 database failure** that drove the AWS migration lasted three days and affected Netflix's ability to ship DVDs (Netflix was primarily a DVD-by-mail service at the time). The root cause was a corrupted Oracle database with no fast recovery path. This single incident motivated the "don't depend on any single system" principle that became the foundation of their architecture.

**Password sharing crackdown (2023)** required significant engineering: device fingerprinting to identify household boundaries, behavioral clustering to distinguish travelers from sharers, account-sharing detection via IP location diversity, and a new account type (paid sharing) that let subscribers add extra members. The infrastructure changes to detect sharing at 238M subscriber scale — without generating false positives on legitimate travelers — was a multi-year engineering project.

**AV1 transition:** Netflix began migrating to AV1 codec in 2020. AV1 offers ~20% better compression than H.265 at equivalent quality. The challenge: encoding a 2-hour movie in AV1 takes 100–1000× longer than H.264 encoding. Netflix built a distributed encoding system that parallelizes encoding across thousands of EC2 instances, processes the backlog continuously, and prioritizes recently released titles (which have the highest watch rates and thus highest ROI from bandwidth savings).
