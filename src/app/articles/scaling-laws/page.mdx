# Neural Scaling Laws: Chinchilla and the Compute-Optimal Frontier

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
Language model loss follows power laws with respect to model size, dataset size, and compute — with no observed saturation across many orders of magnitude. This means you can predict, before training, how good a model will be. The Chinchilla paper showed that GPT-3 was dramatically under-trained: the same FLOPs that trained 175B parameters on 300B tokens would produce a better model at 70B parameters trained on 1.4T tokens. Most models trained before 2022 made the same mistake.
</Callout>

## The Kaplan Scaling Laws (2020)

**Kaplan et al. (OpenAI, 2020)** trained 73 language models ranging from 768 parameters to 1.5 billion, varying compute, dataset size, and model size independently. They found clean power law relationships:

**Loss vs. parameters** (infinite data):
$$L(N) \approx \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \quad \alpha_N \approx 0.076$$

**Loss vs. dataset size** (optimal model size):
$$L(D) \approx \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D}, \quad \alpha_D \approx 0.095$$

**Loss vs. compute** (joint optimization):
$$L(C) \approx \left(\frac{C_c}{C}\right)^{\alpha_C}, \quad \alpha_C \approx 0.050$$

These are log-linear relationships — on a log-log plot, loss and compute form a straight line. The exponents are small: doubling compute reduces loss by only $2^{0.05} - 1 \approx 3.5\%$. Progress is slow but extremely predictable.

**The critical takeaway from Kaplan**: given a fixed compute budget, you should scale model size much faster than dataset size. The optimal dataset size scales as $D_{\text{opt}} \propto N^{0.74}$ — sublinearly in model size. This led to the strategy of training very large models (GPT-3 at 175B) on relatively small datasets (300B tokens).

## Chinchilla: The Kaplan Laws Were Wrong

**Hoffmann et al. (DeepMind, 2022)** ran a much more careful scaling experiment. Instead of varying one variable at a time, they trained over 400 models from 70M to 16B parameters on 5B to 500B tokens, fitting a joint loss model:

$$L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$$

Where $E$ is the irreducible entropy of the data, and the two power law terms represent the contributions of finite model size and finite data. Fitting this model gave $\alpha \approx \beta \approx 0.34$ — crucially, **parameters and tokens should scale equally**.

The compute-optimal model size and token count given a budget $C$ FLOPs:

$$N_{\text{opt}} = G \cdot \left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{0.5}, \quad D_{\text{opt}} = \frac{C}{6 N_{\text{opt}}}$$

The practical rule of thumb: train for approximately **20 tokens per parameter**. A 70B parameter model should train on ~1.4 trillion tokens.

By this standard, GPT-3 (175B params, 300B tokens = 1.7 tokens/param) was trained on 10× too little data. DeepMind trained **Chinchilla** (70B params, 1.4T tokens) with the same FLOPs as Gopher (280B params, 300B tokens) and Chinchilla beat Gopher on every benchmark tested.

<Callout type="info" title="Why Kaplan Was Wrong">
The Kaplan 2020 experiments varied parameters or dataset size but not both jointly — and they trained for a fixed number of steps rather than until convergence on their dataset. This introduced a confound: larger models converge to lower loss in fewer steps, making model size look more important than it is. When Chinchilla researchers trained all models to full convergence on their respective datasets, the relationship between parameters and data became roughly symmetric.
</Callout>

## Post-Chinchilla: Over-Training for Inference

Chinchilla computes the training-optimal frontier — the model that achieves minimum loss for a given training compute budget. But training cost is paid once; inference cost is paid for every request forever.

**Llama (Touvron et al., Meta, 2023)** deliberately violated Chinchilla by training a 7B parameter model on 1 trillion tokens (140 tokens/param — 7× over-trained by Chinchilla standards). The reasoning: a 7B model is cheap to serve. If over-training makes it competitive with larger models, the inference savings vastly outweigh the training overhead.

Result: Llama-7B outperformed GPT-3 (25× more parameters) on most benchmarks. Llama-65B matched Chinchilla-70B despite being the same size — because both were compute-optimal at different points. The Llama approach became standard practice: train smaller models for much longer, making them cheap to deploy.

**The new frontier**: Llama 3.1 (8B) was trained on 15 trillion tokens (1875 tokens/param). DeepSeek-V3 (671B MoE, 37B active) was trained on 14.8T tokens. Both dramatically exceed Chinchilla ratios, reflecting the understanding that inference efficiency matters as much as training efficiency.

## Emergent Abilities

**Wei et al. (2022)** documented a striking phenomenon: some capabilities appear to emerge discontinuously as models scale, rather than improving gradually.

On tasks like **BIG-Bench Hard** (multi-step arithmetic, logical reasoning, causal judgment), models below ~50B parameters achieve near-random performance. Models above ~100B parameters suddenly solve these tasks. The improvement is not gradual — it's a phase transition.

Examples of emerging capabilities:
- **Chain-of-thought reasoning**: 8B models produce worse results with chain-of-thought prompting than without (it confuses the model). 100B+ models produce better results with it.
- **In-context learning**: few-shot examples reliably improve performance only above ~10B parameters.
- **Instruction following**: following complex multi-step instructions generalizes well only above ~50B parameters.
- **Calibration**: models accurately estimate their own uncertainty (know what they don't know) only at large scale.

The mechanistic understanding of emergence remains incomplete. **Schaeffer et al. (2023)** argued that many "emergent" behaviors are artifacts of discontinuous evaluation metrics — measured with a smooth metric, the same capabilities improve gradually. This debate continues.

## Compute Budgets for Frontier Models

Extrapolating from the scaling laws, the training compute of frontier models (estimated from reported chip-hours or context):

| Model | Parameters | Training tokens | Estimated FLOPs |
|-------|------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| GPT-3 (2020) | 175B | 300B | 3.1×10²³ |
| Chinchilla (2022) | 70B | 1.4T | 5.8×10²³ |
| GPT-4 (2023, est.) | ~1.8T MoE | ~13T | ~2×10²⁵ |
| Llama 3.1 405B (2024) | 405B | 15.6T | ~3.8×10²⁵ |
| DeepSeek-V3 (2024) | 671B MoE | 14.8T | 5.6×10²³* |

*DeepSeek's cost is low because MoE active parameters are only 37B — FLOPs scale with active params.

The scaling law predicts that the next 10× increase in training compute produces roughly $10^{0.05} \approx 12\%$ improvement in loss — significant but not transformative. Researchers believe we are approaching the limit of what pure scaling achieves, motivating architectural innovations (MoE, SSMs) and data quality improvements (synthetic data generation, careful curation).

## Data Quality as the New Frontier

Scaling laws assume training on fixed-quality data. Improving data quality shifts the entire curve:

**FineWeb (HuggingFace, 2024)**: 15T token dataset from Common Crawl with aggressive quality filtering. Models trained on FineWeb outperform those trained on 3× more lower-quality data. **Effective data quality multiplies the apparent scaling constant**.

**Synthetic data**: models trained on GPT-4-generated reasoning traces (Phi-3, Llama-3.1 instruct) exhibit capabilities disproportionate to their size. A 3.8B Phi-3-mini trained on synthetic "textbook quality" data matches Llama-2-70B on many benchmarks — effectively multiplying data quality through generation.

**Mixture of Experts**: MoE models use the same FLOPs as dense models but have more total parameters, effectively moving down the parameter scaling law curve for the same compute cost. DeepSeek-V3's 671B total / 37B active parameter design achieves GPT-4 class performance at $6M training cost.

## Practice

<Callout type="info">
Reproduce Chinchilla scaling on a small scale: train character-level language models on Shakespeare at 3 parameter counts (500K, 5M, 50M) with 3 dataset sizes (1M, 10M, 100M tokens) — 9 combinations total. Plot loss on a log-log grid. You should see the power law form clearly and be able to identify which (params, data) combination is compute-optimal for each compute budget.
