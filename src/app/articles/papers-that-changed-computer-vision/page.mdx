# Papers That Changed Computer Vision

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
Computer Vision evolved from researchers writing manual algorithms for edge-detection to deep convolutional networks that learn features automatically. The last decade has been defined by a relentless push for deeper networks, culminating in the replacement of convolutions entirely.
</Callout>

## 2012: AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al.)
**"ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks"**

For decades, AI researchers argued about whether Neural Networks were a dead end. In 2012, AlexNet shattered those doubts by winning the ImageNet competition by a massive, unheard-of 10.8% margin. 

AlexNet wasn't fundamentally new mathematics; it was the application of 5 Convolutional layers, ReLU activations, and Dropout, trained on a massive dataset using **GPUs**. This paper single-handedly ignited the modern Deep Learning boom, proving that raw compute and data trumped hand-crafted feature engineering (like SIFT or HOG).

## 2014: VGG and GoogLeNet
**"Very Deep Convolutional Networks for Large-Scale Image Recognition" (VGG)**
**"Going Deeper with Convolutions" (GoogLeNet)**

After AlexNet, the race was on to build deeper networks. 
- **VGG** (from Oxford) proved that you didn't need complex, varying filter sizes. By stacking dozens of simple, uniform $3 \times 3$ convolutions, you could achieve massive receptive fields and better accuracy.
- **GoogLeNet** introduced the Inception module. Instead of choosing between a $3 \times 3$ or $5 \times 5$ filter, it applied all of them in parallel and concatenated the results, keeping computational costs low while pushing the network to 22 layers deep.

## 2015: ResNet (He et al.)
**"Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition"**

Researchers hit a wall: if you build a network deeper than 30 layers, the accuracy actually gets *worse*. This was due to the vanishing gradient problem.

Kaiming He and his team introduced the **Residual Block**. By adding a simple "skip connection" that bypasses the convolutional layers and adds the original input directly to the output ($F(x) + x$), gradients could flow unimpeded backward through the network. This incredibly simple trick allowed networks to successfully scale to 152 layers and beyond, becoming the default architecture for almost all computer vision tasks for the next five years.

## 2017: FPN and Mask R-CNN
**"Feature Pyramid Networks for Object Detection"**

Classifying a single image is easy; drawing bounding boxes around multiple overlapping objects of different sizes is hard. **FPN** solved the scale problem by creating a pyramid of features, allowing the network to combine low-resolution, highly semantic features (to identify a dog) with high-resolution, low-semantic features (to find the dog's exact edges).

This architecture was immediately utilized by **Mask R-CNN**, which extended bounding-box detection into instance segmentation—literally generating a pixel-perfect mask for every distinct object in a crowded scene.

## 2020: Vision Transformer (Dosovitskiy et al.)
**"An Image is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale"**

For eight years, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) were the undisputed kings of vision. Then, Google researchers applied the NLP Transformer directly to images.

The **Vision Transformer (ViT)** chops an image into a grid of $16 \times 16$ patches, flattens them, and treats them exactly like words in a sentence. While CNNs have an inductive bias assuming that pixels close to each other are related, ViTs have no such bias. They must learn everything from scratch using global self-attention. When trained on massive datasets (JFT-300M), ViTs crushed the best CNNs, proving that scale and generic architectures often win out over domain-specific designs.
