# Neural Architecture Search: When AI Builds AI

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
Instead of human researchers manually designing neural network layers by trial and error, Neural Architecture Search (NAS) treats the architecture itself as a learnable parameter. NAS algorithms automatically search the vast space of possible network designs to find the optimal balance of accuracy, speed, and size.
</Callout>

## The Brute Force Era: RL-based NAS

The earliest successful NAS approaches relied on Reinforcement Learning (RL). A recurrent neural network "controller" would sequentially generate architectural hyperparameters (e.g., filter sizes, strides, number of layers). The generated architecture (a "child" network) was then trained from scratch on a dataset. The resulting validation accuracy was used as the reward signal to update the controller via policy gradient.

This approach produced **NASNet**, which achieved state-of-the-art results on ImageNet. However, the computational cost was staggering. Finding NASNet required **800 GPUs running for 28 days** (over 22,000 GPU-hours) just to search for a single architecture block.

## Differentiable Search: DARTS

To make NAS computationally viable, researchers realized they needed to stop training thousands of discrete networks from scratch. **DARTS** (Differentiable Architecture Search) introduced a continuous relaxation of the architecture space.

Instead of choosing exactly one operation (e.g., $3 \times 3$ conv OR $5 \times 5$ conv), DARTS computes a weighted sum of *all* possible operations. The weights (denoted as $\alpha$) for these operations are optimized using standard gradient descent alongside the network's normal weights. At the end of the search, the operation with the highest $\alpha$ weight is kept, and the rest are pruned.

This continuous optimization reduced search time from thousands of GPU-days down to just **4 GPU-days**, democratizing architecture search for researchers without mega-clusters.

## Compound Scaling: EfficientNet

Sometimes, the search space isn't every individual layer, but rather how to scale a known good baseline. **EfficientNet** utilized NAS to find a strong baseline network (EfficientNet-B0) optimized for both accuracy and FLOPS. 

The authors then observed that scaling a network's depth, width, and resolution independently is suboptimal. They introduced a **compound scaling factor** ($\phi$) that uniformly scales network width, depth, and resolution in a principled way. By simply increasing $\phi$, they generated the EfficientNet B1-B7 family, achieving SOTA accuracy on ImageNet with significantly fewer parameters than competitors like ResNet or Inception.

## Once-for-All (OFA) Supernets

Deploying models across thousands of different devices (from high-end GPUs to IoT microcontrollers) traditionally required running a separate NAS search for each hardware target's specific latency constraints.

**Once-for-All (OFA)** changed this paradigm. It trains a single, massive "supernet" that contains billions of smaller sub-networks. Due to a progressive shrinking training strategy, any sub-network drawn from the supernet inherits its weights and is fully functional *without any additional training*. 

To deploy to a new phone, you simply run an evolutionary search over the supernet to find the sub-network that hits the latency target. This decouples training from searching, resulting in a **1000× reduction** in search cost for multi-device deployment.

## Zero-Cost Proxies

Even with supernets, evaluating thousands of sub-networks takes time. **Zero-cost proxies** are a recent innovation where a network's potential accuracy is estimated *at initialization*, without training it for a single epoch. Metrics like `synflow` or `jacob_cov` analyze the flow of gradients through the untrained network to score its topological expressivity, allowing NAS algorithms to evaluate architectures in milliseconds.
