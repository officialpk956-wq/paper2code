# LLM Evaluation: MMLU, HumanEval, and the Benchmark Problem

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
Language model benchmarks measure proxies, not the thing you actually want. MMLU measures multiple-choice knowledge recall. HumanEval measures whether code passes unit tests. MT-Bench measures whether GPT-4 prefers one response over another. None of these directly measure whether the model will be useful in production. Yet benchmark performance is the primary signal used to compare models. Understanding what each benchmark measures — and what it misses — is essential for interpreting LLM claims.
</Callout>

## Perplexity: The Foundation Metric

**Perplexity** is the oldest and most theoretically principled language model metric: the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood on a test set.

$$\text{PPL}(W) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P(w_i | w_{<i})\right)$$

Intuitively: perplexity measures how "surprised" the model is by the test text. A perplexity of 10 means the model is as uncertain as if it had to choose among 10 equally likely words at each step. Lower perplexity = better model.

Perplexity advantages: differentiable, dataset-independent (comparable across models on the same test set), sensitive to small model improvements. Perplexity limitations: doesn't measure usefulness (a model can have low perplexity while being useless for instruction following), sensitive to tokenization (models with different tokenizers are not directly comparable), and doesn't capture rare failure modes.

## Knowledge and Reasoning: MMLU

**MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding, Hendrycks et al., 2020)** is the standard benchmark for knowledge and academic reasoning: 57 subjects covering STEM, humanities, social sciences, professional domains (medicine, law). Each question is 4-choice multiple choice. Scoring is accuracy (fraction of correct answers).

Subjects range from elementary mathematics and world history to US foreign policy, clinical knowledge, college physics, and professional law. A human expert is estimated at 89.8% overall; random guessing gives 25%.

**How models are evaluated**: typically 5-shot (5 example questions in the prompt to teach the format). The model must output one of A/B/C/D. Some evaluations use log-probabilities to select the highest-probability option rather than generative output.

MMLU benchmark scores at the time of release:
- GPT-3 (175B, 2020): 43.9%
- GPT-3.5 (2022): 70.0%
- GPT-4 (2023): 86.4%
- Llama-3.1 405B (2024): 88.6%
- DeepSeek-V3 (2024): 88.5%

## Code Evaluation: HumanEval and SWE-bench

**HumanEval (Chen et al., OpenAI, 2021)**: 164 Python programming problems. Each problem has a function signature, docstring, and unit tests. The model generates the function body; correctness is measured by passing all unit tests.

The primary metric is **pass@k**: the probability that at least one of k generated samples passes all tests. pass@1 uses k=1 (single attempt). pass@10 generates 10 samples and checks if any passes.

HumanEval scores:
- GPT-3.5 (2022): 48.1% pass@1
- GPT-4 (2023): 67.0% pass@1
- DeepSeek-Coder-V2 (2024): 90.2% pass@1
- Claude 3.5 Sonnet (2024): 92.0% pass@1

**MBPP (Mostly Basic Programming Problems)**: 374 entry-level Python problems. More diverse than HumanEval, includes text processing, math, and algorithmic tasks.

**SWE-bench (2023)**: 2294 real GitHub issues from 12 Python repositories. The model must produce a code patch that resolves the issue and passes the associated test suite. Much harder than HumanEval: requires reading codebases, understanding context, and producing multi-file diffs.

SWE-bench pass rates (as of late 2024): ~30–40% for leading models — a much harder task than HumanEval's near-100% scores suggest.

## Reasoning: BIG-Bench and BIG-Bench Hard

**BIG-Bench (2022)**: 204 tasks covering diverse capabilities not captured by MMLU: code generation, math word problems, logical reasoning, social reasoning, understanding of metaphor, creative writing evaluation, and more. Tasks contributed by 450+ researchers.

**BIG-Bench Hard (BBH)**: a subset of 23 tasks where prior models (2022) performed below human average. Includes:
- Multi-step arithmetic (e.g., track object counts through a series of operations)
- Logical deduction (given clues, determine ordering of objects)
- Causal judgment (which event caused which?)
- Formal fallacies (identify whether an argument is valid)
- Date understanding (add/subtract days from dates)

BBH is particularly useful because it requires multi-step reasoning, not just recall. Models without chain-of-thought prompting often score near random on BBH; with CoT, scores improve dramatically — making it a good benchmark for reasoning capability.

<Callout type="warning" title="Benchmark Contamination">
All widely used benchmarks are suspect. Training data for large models is scraped from the web, and MMLU, HumanEval, and GSM8K have been publicly available since 2020–2021. A model trained on web data after 2021 has likely seen the test questions in its training corpus — either directly or in blog posts discussing them.

Several studies have demonstrated contamination: models score higher when test questions are presented verbatim versus paraphrased. The actual intelligence being measured is unclear. New benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, HardMath) and private test sets are attempts to address this, but the fundamental problem remains: any published benchmark will eventually be contaminated.
</Callout>

## Instruction Following: MT-Bench and LLM-as-Judge

**MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023)**: 80 multi-turn conversation questions across 8 domains (writing, roleplay, extraction, reasoning, math, coding, STEM, humanities). Each question has 2 turns. GPT-4 is used as the judge: it grades responses on a scale of 1–10.

The LLM-as-judge approach enables evaluation of open-ended responses that can't be scored by unit tests or multiple-choice matching. GPT-4 agreement with human judges is ~80%, comparable to human-human agreement (~80%).

**Chatbot Arena (LMSYS)**: crowdsourced pairwise comparisons. Users chat with two anonymous models and vote on which was better. Models are ranked using the Elo rating system (same as chess). Chatbot Arena has the strongest claim to measuring what users actually want because:
- Judged by real users on real tasks they care about
- Blind comparison eliminates brand bias
- Millions of data points across diverse prompts
- Elo rating is a principled comparison system

Arena ranking often diverges from benchmark ranking: models that score highest on MMLU don't necessarily win Arena votes.

## Math Benchmarks: GSM8K and MATH

**GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021)**: 8,500 elementary math word problems requiring 2–8 steps. Designed to be solvable by 3rd–8th graders. Scores: GPT-3 (0% with standard prompting, 35% with CoT), GPT-4 (92%), Llama-3.1-8B (84.4%).

**MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021)**: 12,500 competition math problems (AMC, AIME, etc.) across 7 difficulty levels. Far harder than GSM8K. Scores: GPT-4 initial (42%), GPT-4 with code (68%), o1-mini (90%), DeepSeek-R1 (97.3%).

**AIME 2024**: 30 American Invitational Mathematics Examination problems. Problems unseen during training (released in 2024). o1-mini: 56.7%, DeepSeek-R1: 79.8%. AIME is used specifically because it's recent and harder to contaminate.

## Evaluation Design Principles

**Contamination avoidance**: use test sets released after the model's training cutoff. Include problems that require generating novel solutions, not recalling memorized ones.

**Multiple metrics**: no single benchmark captures overall capability. Report MMLU (knowledge), HumanEval (code), MATH (reasoning), MT-Bench (instruction following), Arena (user preference) together.

**Compute-matched comparison**: a model with 2× more parameters will generally score better. Compare models at the same FLOP budget for fair assessment.

**Task-specific evaluation**: for production use, benchmark on your actual task with your actual data. A model that ranks 5th on MMLU might rank 1st on your specific domain (legal, medical, financial).

## Practice

<Callout type="info">
Run a mini-benchmark yourself: pick 20 questions from a domain you know well (not from public datasets), ask three models the same questions, and evaluate manually. You'll quickly find that benchmark rankings don't predict which model answers your specific questions well — and that your domain expertise catches errors the models confabulate confidently. This is the most important evaluation insight: benchmark your models on your use case, not on published leaderboards.
</Callout>
