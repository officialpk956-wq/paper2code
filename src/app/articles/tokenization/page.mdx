# Tokenization: BPE, WordPiece, and Why Tokens Matter

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
Language models don't see words — they see tokens. "tokenization" might be one token, three tokens ("token", "ization"), or five tokens ("t", "ok", "en", "iz", "ation") depending on the tokenizer. The choice of tokenization algorithm affects what tasks are hard for the model, how many tokens fit in the context window, and why models fail at seemingly simple tasks like counting letters or reversing strings. Tokenization is the invisible layer between language and computation.
</Callout>

## Why Not Just Use Characters or Words?

**Character-level models**: every character is a token. Pros: vocabulary size is tiny (~256), can handle any text. Cons: sequences are very long (100 characters = 100 tokens), and the model must learn all linguistic structure from character patterns — very data inefficient.

**Word-level models**: every word is a token. Pros: short sequences, tokens are semantically meaningful. Cons: vocabulary is huge (English has 170K+ words), out-of-vocabulary words (new proper nouns, technical terms, other languages) require special handling, and morphologically related words ("run", "runs", "running", "runner") are separate with no shared representation.

**Subword tokenization**: the middle ground. Common words are single tokens; rare words are split into meaningful subword units. "tokenization" → ["token", "ization"]. The vocabulary (typically 32K–100K tokens) covers almost all text efficiently, sequences are shorter than character-level, and morphological relationships are captured (the "ization" suffix appears in many words, so the model learns what it means).

## Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)

**BPE (Sennrich et al., 2015)** was originally a data compression algorithm. Applied to text:

1. Start with the vocabulary of individual characters (all 256 bytes for byte-level BPE)
2. Count all pairs of adjacent tokens in the training corpus
3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token
4. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size

Example with target vocabulary size 10:

```
Corpus: "low low low lower lower newest newest"

Initial: {l, o, w, e, r, n, s, t, space}

Step 1: most frequent pair is (l, o) → merge to "lo"
Step 2: most frequent pair is (lo, w) → merge to "low"
Step 3: most frequent pair is (low, er) → merge to "lower"
Step 4: most frequent pair is (n, e) → merge to "ne"
...
```

BPE is **greedy** — each step merges the single most frequent pair. The final vocabulary contains the most common subwords in the training corpus. **GPT-2, GPT-4, LLaMA, and most modern LLMs use BPE or byte-level BPE** (BPE applied to bytes rather than characters, which handles any Unicode text including emojis, Chinese, Arabic, etc. without special handling).

## WordPiece and Unigram

**WordPiece (Schuster & Nakamura, 2012, used in BERT)** is similar to BPE but merges based on the maximum increase in the likelihood of the training data, not raw frequency. The merge criterion is:

$$\text{score}(a, b) = \frac{\text{freq}(ab)}{\text{freq}(a) \times \text{freq}(b)}$$

This favors merges that are statistically surprising — pairs that appear together much more often than chance. The result is slightly different tokenization than BPE, with better handling of rare subwords.

**Unigram Language Model tokenization (Kudo, 2018, used in SentencePiece)** takes the opposite approach: start with a large vocabulary and iteratively remove tokens that increase the training loss least. The result is optimized via a language model objective rather than a greedy merge rule.

**SentencePiece** (also by Kudo, used in T5, LLaMA, and many multilingual models) is an implementation that runs tokenization on raw Unicode text without pre-tokenization by spaces. This is important for languages like Japanese and Chinese that don't use spaces between words.

## How Tokenization Affects Model Behavior

**Arithmetic**: "257 + 843 = " depends on whether numbers are tokenized as single tokens or digit-by-digit. GPT-4's tokenizer tends to split numbers with more than 3 digits into multiple tokens. The model must then learn to manipulate tokens that don't correspond to meaningful numerical units — explaining why arithmetic is much harder for LLMs than for symbolic systems.

**Counting letters**: "How many 'r's are in 'strawberry'?" The model sees "str", "aw", "berry" — separate tokens. The letters within tokens are not individually accessible. The model must somehow "look inside" tokens, which it was never trained to do directly. This is why models famously fail at counting letters.

**Token efficiency by language**: English has short words and large training corpora — most common English words are single tokens. Other languages may have longer words, more complex morphology, or smaller training representation, leading to more tokens per concept. A 4K context window might fit 3,000 English words but only 800 Arabic words — affecting what tasks are feasible in context.

<Callout type="warning" title="The Tokenization Tax on Non-English Languages">
Most LLMs are trained primarily on English text, so their vocabularies are optimized for English. A single English word like "the" is one token; its translation "les" in French is typically also one token; but the Indonesian translation "yang" might be split into two tokens on tokenizers optimized for English. More tokens per concept means: less fits in the context window, inference is more expensive, and the model has more "steps" of computation per word — which affects quality for low-resource languages.
</Callout>

## The Vocabulary Size Tradeoff

**Small vocabulary** (e.g., 8K tokens): short sequences, but rare words need many tokens. Less memory for the embedding table. Works poorly for multilingual or domain-specific text.

**Large vocabulary** (e.g., 256K tokens): most words are single tokens, sequences are short. But the embedding table and output projection layer are large — for vocabulary size $V$ and dimension $d$, these add $2Vd$ parameters. For $V=256K$ and $d=4096$: 2 billion parameters just for embeddings.

Modern models use 32K–128K token vocabularies:
- GPT-2: 50,257 tokens
- LLaMA 2: 32,000 tokens
- LLaMA 3: 128,256 tokens (4× increase for better multilingual handling)
- GPT-4: ~100,000 tokens (estimated)

LLaMA 3's vocabulary increase to 128K significantly improved performance on code (code-specific tokens) and multilingual text (more non-English subwords in vocabulary).

## Tokenization Quirks

**Special tokens**: all modern tokenizers include special tokens for formatting: `<|endoftext|>` (GPT), `[CLS]` and `[SEP]` (BERT), `<s>` and `</s>` (LLaMA). These are added artificially and have special meaning — the model learns to associate `[CLS]` with classification tasks, `<|endoftext|>` with document boundaries.

**Chat templates**: instruction-following models use tokenizer templates to format conversations:
```
<|begin_of_text|><|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>
Hello<|eot_id|><|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>
```
These templates are crucial — using the wrong template with an instruction-tuned model produces garbage output.

**Tokenization inconsistency**: "New York" might tokenize to `["New", " York"]` while " New York" (with a leading space) might tokenize to `[" New", " York"]`. The leading space creates a different token. This can cause surprising model behavior when the same text appears at different positions in the context.

Explore tokenization directly with `tiktoken` (OpenAI's tokenizer library): tokenize the same sentence in English, French, Arabic, and Chinese and compare token counts. Then tokenize numbers (1, 12, 123, 1234, 12345) and observe when numbers split into multiple tokens. Finally, tokenize code in Python vs. JavaScript — Python tends to tokenize more efficiently because of its larger representation in training data. These experiments take 10 minutes and build lasting intuition about where LLMs struggle.
