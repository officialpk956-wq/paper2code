# Papers That Changed NLP

<Callout type="tip" title="Key Insight">
The transition of NLP from rigid linguistic rules to flexible, deep-learning-based intelligence didn't happen overnight. It was driven by a sequence of breakthrough papers, each solving a critical bottleneck in how machines represent, process, and generate human language.
</Callout>

## 2013: Word2Vec (Mikolov et al.)
**"Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space"**

Before 2013, words were treated as discrete, independent symbols (one-hot encoding). The word "cat" had no mathematical relationship to "dog". 

Google's Word2Vec introduced dense, low-dimensional embeddings. By training a shallow neural network to predict a word based on its surrounding context (Continuous Bag-of-Words) or predict the context based on a word (Skip-Gram), the model learned that words appearing in similar contexts share similar meanings. It produced the famous algebraic relationship: `Vector("King") - Vector("Man") + Vector("Woman") ≈ Vector("Queen")`.

## 2014: Seq2Seq (Sutskever et al.)
**"Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks"**

Machine translation used to require complex, hand-crafted statistical alignment models. This paper proved that you could map an input sequence (English) to an output sequence (French) using two Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).

An **Encoder RNN** reads the English sentence word-by-word and compresses its entire meaning into a single, fixed-size vector (the hidden state). A **Decoder RNN** then takes that vector and generates the French sentence word-by-word. This end-to-end approach became the standard for all generative NLP tasks.

## 2015: Attention (Bahdanau et al.)
**"Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate"**

The flaw with the 2014 Seq2Seq model was the "information bottleneck." Forcing a massive 50-word paragraph into a single fixed-size vector caused the network to forget the beginning of the sentence.

Bahdanau introduced **Attention**. Instead of relying on a single final vector, the Decoder was allowed to look back at *all* the hidden states of the Encoder. At every step of generating the French translation, the network dynamically learned to "pay attention" only to the relevant English words (e.g., focusing on "pomme" when translating "apple").

## 2017: The Transformer (Vaswani et al.)
**"Attention Is All You Need"**

RNNs process text sequentially (word 1, then word 2). This makes them impossible to parallelize on GPUs.

Google researchers asked: What if we throw away the RNN entirely and *only* use Attention? The resulting **Transformer** architecture processed the entire sentence at once in parallel. By relying on Multi-Head Self-Attention, words could instantly gather context from anywhere in the document. This broke the sequential processing bottleneck, allowing models to scale to billions of parameters.

## 2018: BERT (Devlin et al.)
**"BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding"**

Early Transformers only looked left-to-right. **BERT** introduced the Masked Language Modeling objective. By masking out random words in a sentence and forcing the model to predict them using context from *both* the left and the right, BERT achieved deep, bidirectional understanding. It set new records on almost every NLP benchmark and proved the value of massive, unsupervised pre-training followed by task-specific fine-tuning.

## 2019-2020: GPT-2 and GPT-3 (Brown et al.)
**"Language Models are Few-Shot Learners"**

While BERT dominated text classification, OpenAI focused on text generation with the **GPT** series (Generative Pre-trained Transformer). GPT-3, with its massive 175 billion parameters, proved that if you make an autoregressive model large enough, you don't even need to fine-tune it. It exhibited **In-Context Learning**—capable of performing entirely new tasks simply by reading a few examples in the prompt (Few-Shot prompting).

## Practice

<Callout type="info">
The best way to understand NLP history is to experience the leap in capabilities. Find an old RNN-based text generation script (like a character-level LSTM) and train it on Shakespeare. Then, run inference on a modern Transformer (like GPT-2) prompted with Shakespeare. Compare the grammatical coherence and long-term memory of the outputs.
</Callout>
